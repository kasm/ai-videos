#The Forest Canopy

Chapter I of The Wind in the Willows, adapted to the deep woods (originally by Kenneth Grahame, 1908).

The Mole had been working all morning, spring-cleaning his little home,
till he had dust in his throat, splashes of whitewash all over his black
fur, and an aching back. Spring was penetrating even his dark house with
its spirit of divine discontent. Suddenly he flung down his brush, cried
“Hang spring-cleaning!” and bolted up his steep little tunnel, scraping
and scrabbling until, pop! his snout came out into the dappled sunlight
of a great oak glade.

“This is fine!” he said, jumping off all four legs in the joy of living.
He trotted across the moss to a bramble thicket, where an elderly rabbit
demanded, “Sixpence for passing by the private path!” The impatient Mole
bowled him over, jeered “Acorn-sauce!” at the peeping rabbits, and
rambled on through fern-brakes and clearings, delighted to be the only
idle fellow among all the busy, nest-building citizens.

Suddenly he stood at the foot of an ancient, colossal oak. Never had he
seen the Deep Forest Canopy before—this vast, soaring cathedral of
living timber, swaying and sighing, tossing shadows and catching
sunbeams. Bewitched, he sat upon a mossy root while the boughs murmured
stories drawn up from the heart of the earth.

Gazing upward, he spied a dark hollow in a neighboring beech. Inside,
something twinkled like a tiny star, then winked, and a small russet
face grew up around it.

A pert round face, with whiskers.

Tufted neat ears and a plumed, shadowy tail.

It was the Red Squirrel!

“Hullo, Mole!” said the Red Squirrel. “Would you like to come up?”

“Oh, it’s all very well to talk,” said the Mole pettishly.

The Squirrel unfastened a woven vine and lowered a little basket-car of
hazel-withies, lined with moss and rigged to counterweights and pulleys.
He helped the Mole inside and hauled them smoothly up into the branches.

“I’ve never been up in the trees before in all my life,” gasped the Mole
in rapture.

“Believe me, my young friend,” said the Squirrel solemnly, “there is
nothing half so much worth doing as simply messing about in the
treetops. Simply messing—about—in—the——”

“Look above, Squirrel!” cried the Mole.

Too late. The basket struck a stout limb full tilt, tipping the Squirrel
onto his back with his paws in the air.

“—treetops—or with branches,” the Squirrel continued composedly,
picking himself up with a laugh. “Whether you reach the crown or never
get anywhere at all, you’re always busy, and you never do anything in
particular. Supposing we travel along the high boughs together and have
a long day of it?”

The Mole waggled his toes in bliss. The Squirrel fetched a fat wicker
luncheon-basket from his hollow, shoved it under Mole’s feet, and swung
the car out along a horizontal ropeway.

“What’s inside?” asked the Mole.

“Roasted chestnuts,” replied the Squirrel briefly; “
hazelnutspinenutsbeechmastpickledacornshoneycombwildberriesbirchsap
elderflowerwine——”

“O stop, stop!” cried the Mole in ecstacies. Intoxicated with the green
light and pine-resin scents, he trailed a paw through the crisp leaves.
“So—this—is—a—Forest!”

“The Forest,” corrected the Squirrel. “It’s my world, and I don’t want
any other. Whether autumn gales rock my drey like a ship at sea, or
spring snows melt to show bark smelling like spiced cake, it’s always
got its fun. And it’s crowded enough—woodpeckers, jays, nuthatches,
always wanting you to do something!”

“What lies over there?” asked the Mole, pointing toward a dark tangle
of ancient yews and thorns.

“Just the Deep Thicket,” said the Squirrel shortly. “We high-canopy folk
don’t go there much. Dear old Badger lives in the roots—nobody interferes
with him. As for the weasels, stoats, and foxes—you can’t really trust
them. And beyond the Thicket comes the Wide World, which doesn’t matter
to either of us. Now, here’s our mossy platform for lunch!”

They moored in an emerald bower high in an ancient elm, beside a rustling
aspen. The Mole unpacked the leaf-cloth and packets with gasps of “O my!”
and they fell to eating. Presently, Mole noticed a streak of swaying
twigs below. The Squirrel chattered an invitation, and a sleek Pine
Marten hauled himself onto the bough.

“Greedy beggars!” said the Marten, joining them. “All the world seems
out in the trees today.”

Below, a stripy head peered from the ferns. “Come on up, old Badger!”
shouted the Squirrel. Badger merely grunted, “H’m! Company,” and
vanished.

“Simply hates Society,” sighed the Squirrel. “Well, who’s out in the
woods?”

“Toad’s out,” said the Marten, “in his brand-new gliding-contraption!”
They laughed over Toad’s passing crazes—tree-houses, zip-lines,
hammock-camping—just as a canvas glider flashed past the clearing,
banking wildly. “He’ll be out of that in a minute,” chuckled the Marten.
“Did I ever tell you about Toad and the forest-warden? Toad....”

An errant wood-beetle buzzed past. A flash of russet fur, a “snap!”—and
both beetle and Marten were gone, leaving only trembling leaves below.
Knowing animal-etiquette forbade commenting on sudden disappearances,
the Mole simply repacked the basket, forgetting only a leaf-plate, a
wooden fork, and the honey-pot he had been sitting on.

As the afternoon sun dipped, the Mole grew restless. “Squirrelly!
Please, I want to work the hoist-rope now!”

“Not yet, my young friend,” smiled the Squirrel. “Wait till you’ve had
lessons.”

Jealous and proud, the Mole suddenly jumped up and grabbed the haul-line.
The startled Squirrel tumbled backwards; the Mole gave a mighty yank,
unhooked the brake, and—Whoosh! Over tipped the car, and the Mole
plummeted through the branches.

Leaves whipped his ears as he fell down, down, catching a slender hazel
bough just as it began to crack. A firm paw gripped his neck—it was the
Squirrel, shaking with laughter. Using a trailing vine, the Squirrel
lowered the bruised, leaf-strewn Mole to the moss, then climbed back up
to right the car and rescue the wedged luncheon-basket.

Back in the hoisted basket, the dejected Mole choked out, “Squirrelly,
my generous friend! I am very sorry for my foolish conduct. I have been
a complete ass. Will you forgive me?”

“Bless you, what’s a little tumble to a Red Squirrel?” said his friend
cheerily. “Come and stop with me awhile. I’ll teach you to climb and
balance on a bough, and you’ll soon be as handy in the canopy as any of
us.”

Touched, the Mole wiped away a tear, and soon revived enough to trade
back-talk with two sniggering jays.

At home, the Squirrel lit a bright resin-lamp, wrapped the Mole in a
leaf-robe before the fire, and told thrilling woodland tales of hawks,
woodcutters, and night-foragings until supper. Afterward, the sleepy
Mole was escorted to the best bedroom-hollow, laying his head on a
moss-pillow in deep contentment while the Forest Canopy whispered outside.

That day was the first of many, as the Mole learnt to leap and climb,
listening with his ear to the rough bark to what the wind went
whispering among the leaves.
